SOP and Motivation Letter Guide

Learn how to write a clear, human statement of purpose or motivation letter that connects your evidence, goals and fit to the specific opportunity.

Updated 30 September 2026•OpportunityTrack practical guide

Understand the document you are being asked to write

A statement of purpose and a motivation letter can overlap, but they are not always identical. A statement of purpose often emphasizes academic preparation, research interests and future direction. A motivation letter may put more emphasis on why the specific fellowship, scholarship, project or program matters to you.

Follow the title and questions used by the provider. If a portal asks three separate questions, do not submit one generic essay instead.

Start with a clear reason for applying

The opening should identify the opportunity and explain the connection to your background without dramatic language. A direct opening is often stronger than a quotation or a broad statement about changing the world.

For a research project, mention the scientific or computational question that genuinely interests you. For a scholarship, connect the award to the study or impact you are preparing for.

Use evidence instead of adjectives

Selection committees cannot verify adjectives such as “hardworking,” “passionate” or “excellent” from the word itself. They can evaluate evidence. Describe a project you completed, a problem you solved, a method you learned or a responsibility you handled.

The best evidence is relevant evidence. A technical project can support an application to computational research; leadership in a student initiative may be more relevant to a leadership scholarship.

Explain fit without copying the website

Read the project or program description, identify the methods and goals, then explain how your experience connects. Do not repeat entire phrases from the official page. Your letter should sound like your own reasoning.

If you are entering a new field, say so clearly. Explain the transferable skills you bring and what you need to learn. Honest interdisciplinary motivation is stronger than invented expertise.

Build a simple structure

A practical structure is: why you are applying, what you have done, why this specific opportunity fits, what you can contribute, what you want to learn and what direction you hope to build afterward.

Each paragraph should have a purpose. Remove sentences that could be sent unchanged to any university or fellowship.

Keep the tone human and professional

Professional writing does not need complicated vocabulary. Shorter sentences, concrete verbs and specific examples usually sound more credible than inflated language. Read the letter aloud. If a sentence sounds unlike something you would naturally explain in an interview, simplify it.

Avoid making claims you cannot defend. It is acceptable to say you are still building knowledge in a field when the opportunity welcomes learners.

Edit for clarity

After writing, check whether the letter answers three questions: Why this opportunity? Why you? Why now? Then check grammar, names, project title and file format.

If there is a word limit, respect it. If no limit is provided, one focused page is often more effective than several pages of repetition for many fellowship and internship applications.

Common mistakes to remove

Generic openings, copied program text, unsupported claims, repeating the CV line by line, using the wrong university or lab name, and describing goals without any connection to past evidence are common weaknesses.

Turning your background into a coherent story

Your application history does not need to be perfectly linear. Many applicants move from one subject area to another or discover a research interest through a project. The letter should explain that transition in a way that makes sense. Show the experience that led to the new interest and the skills that make the next step realistic.

When you lack formal experience in a target field, do not hide that gap. Explain the computational, analytical, communication or research habits you already have and identify the knowledge you want to gain. This is especially effective when the opportunity explicitly welcomes interdisciplinary applicants.

Adapting the same experience to different applications

The facts of a project should not change, but the relevant lesson can. A machine-learning project might demonstrate technical implementation in an internship application, data handling in a research application or independent problem solving in a scholarship essay. Tailoring means choosing the relevant emphasis, not rewriting history.

A final human-language test

Before submitting, read the letter aloud and ask whether you could naturally explain the same ideas in an interview. Remove exaggerated phrases, long chains of adjectives and statements that sound impressive but contain no evidence. Clear English usually sounds more confident than unnecessarily complex English.

When the application gives specific questions

Answer the questions directly rather than forcing a prewritten letter into the form. If the portal asks separately about academic preparation, motivation and future goals, divide your evidence accordingly. Repetition across answers wastes limited space.

Using feedback without losing your voice

A reviewer can help identify unclear sentences, missing evidence and grammar problems, but the final letter should still sound like you. If editing turns a simple explanation into language you would never use or introduces claims that are not true, revise it again.

Before you convert to PDF

Check the exact project title, institution, lab, degree and addressee. Search the document for names copied from older applications. Then review paragraph spacing and font size so the page remains easy to read. A professional motivation letter should not require tiny text simply to fit unnecessary repetition.
