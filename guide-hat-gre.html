HAT and GRE Guidance

Understand when HAT or GRE scores matter, how to check program requirements and how to plan focused test preparation.

Updated 30 September 2026•OpportunityTrack practical guide

Take the test because it is required or useful

Standardized tests require time and money, so first confirm whether your target scholarship or university actually requires the score. Some programs make tests optional, some waive them for certain applicants and others use them as a core part of selection.

Create a list of target programs and record each testing requirement before deciding what to prepare.

HAT planning

For opportunities that require HAT, confirm the category, official syllabus or test composition, registration process and score conditions from the relevant authority. Preparation should match the actual test category rather than a generic set of questions.

GRE planning

GRE requirements can vary by department. If a program accepts or requires it, check whether it publishes typical or minimum scores and whether the quantitative section is especially important for your field.

Use a diagnostic test to identify weaknesses, then study concepts and practice under time limits. Track mistakes by type so practice produces information rather than only a score.

Connect preparation to deadlines

Work backward from the application deadline, allowing time for score reporting and a possible retake. Do not schedule the first attempt so late that one unexpected result removes all flexibility.

Keep testing in proportion

A test score is one part of an application. Continue improving your CV, statements, references, projects and program research rather than treating a single score as the whole application.

Create a score requirement table

For each target program, record whether the test is required, optional or not considered, the latest acceptable test date and any published minimum or competitive score information. This avoids preparing for a test that does not help your application while missing another requirement that does.

Practice should produce diagnosis

After each practice set, classify mistakes. Was the concept unfamiliar, did you misread the question, run out of time or make a calculation error? Different mistakes need different solutions. Repeating tests without reviewing errors can create activity without much improvement.

Balance test preparation with the rest of the application

References, statements, transcripts and program research also need time. Build a weekly schedule that protects those tasks. A strong test score cannot repair an incomplete application, and an excellent statement cannot compensate for a mandatory score that was never submitted.

On test day and score reporting

Follow the official test provider’s identification and arrival rules, and understand how scores are reported to institutions. Keep confirmation records. If a program requires official electronic reporting, uploading a screenshot may not satisfy the requirement.

Set a realistic preparation period

Your starting level, target score and weekly study time determine how long preparation may take. A diagnostic test gives you a better basis for planning than an arbitrary number of weeks. Build enough time for review, timed practice and at least one period of consolidation before the test.

Use official registration information

Registration dates, identification rules, test centers, fees and score-reporting procedures can change. Use the official test authority for those details. Third-party preparation websites can help with study, but they should not be your only source for administrative rules.

After the result

Decide on a retake by comparing the score with actual target-program requirements, not with someone else's score. If the current result meets the relevant standard, spending additional months on a small score increase may be less valuable than improving the rest of the application.

Quantitative and verbal preparation should reflect your needs

Applicants from technical backgrounds may need more attention on verbal reasoning or analytical writing, while applicants from other backgrounds may need more quantitative review. Use diagnostic results rather than assumptions about your degree to decide where time is needed.

Build accuracy before speed. Once you can solve a question type reliably, practice under realistic time pressure. Guessing quickly without understanding recurring mistakes usually produces unstable results.

Plan score reporting before the deadline

Programs can differ in how they receive scores and whether unofficial results are accepted temporarily. Check the application instructions well before the deadline so you know whether a testing agency must send an official report and how long that process may take.

Final test-week checklist

In the final week, shift from collecting new material to reviewing recurring mistakes, confirming test-day requirements and completing a small amount of timed practice. Protect sleep and routine rather than attempting to learn an entire syllabus at the last minute.

Keep registration confirmation, identification requirements and travel details ready. If the test is remote, complete the official equipment and environment checks in advance.
